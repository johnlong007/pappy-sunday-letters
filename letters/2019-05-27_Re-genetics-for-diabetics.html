> Since Benjamin got diagnosed with Type 1 diabetes (after Jonathan did as

>
> A brief, not-too detailed explanation of the genetics involved (Christina,
> forgive me if I butcher this.)
> Everyone has an immune system that recognizes “invaders” of various types,
> like viruses. The genes in question code for proteins on the surface of
> immune cells. The larger complex of genes is called the MHC complex and is
> one of the genome areas that varies the most between people, which makes
> sense- as we evolved this system would have been prone to high selection
> pressure after exposure to many different pathogens. So there are many
> variations between these genes between different people. Unfortunately,
> certain types of mutations that may have been useful in the past for some
> reason predispose one to autoimmune diseases where your over-excited immune
> system attacks your own body. Type 1 diabetes happens when it attacks the
> pancreas cells that make insulin. Other common disorders relating to this
> complex are celiac disease and Grave’s disease (which should also sound
> familiar in this family.)
>
> Certain combinations of genes correspond to HLA (human leukocyte antigen)
> types. HLA types have implications for marrow and organ transplantation,
> which makes sense- you don’t want to transplant an organ to someone with a
> non-compatible immune system type. Certain HLA types predispose you to
> certain kinds of autoimmune disorders; the specific disorder depends on the
> type. When you start to form as a baby you get one copy of a gene from your
> mom and one from your dad. Each copy has an “allele” or variation encoded
> in it. A simple example is the cystic fibrosis gene. If Dad has a
> dominant-recessive pattern (Xx) and so does Mom (another Xx) their babies
> can inherit different mixes (XX, xx, Xx, and Xx.) The recessive combination
> (xx) will give the baby cystic fibrosis. All the other ones will not.
> There’s a 25% chance of getting it. The HLA complex is more complex and is
> determined by a LOT of genes and single nucleotide polymorphisms
> (variations between A, G, T, and C) in your DNA. However, researchers have
> figured out ways to use machine learning to predict HLA type based on
> SNPs. I read a whole bunch of research papers on this; it’s an established
> technique and not just something I made up. The flowchart is a variation on
> one I found in a paper.
>
> There exists a strong predisposition to Type 1 diabetes with an HLA type
> present in our family. A simplified version of the genetics involved is in
> the flowchart attached. The variations involved are called for the purposes
> of this email (and much research) “DR3, DR4, and DR x”. 3 and 4 types both
> predispose to diabetes. “x” is code for something else- not a 3 or a 4, but
> something that could predispose you to other things. Homozygous 33 and 44
> both predispose to diabetes, as does ¾. 3x and 4x have a weak
> predisposition. xx has a 1 OR (odds ratio, multiplier by general population
> risk of getting the disease.) In my nuclear family, my dad has a ¾ type and
> my mom has a 3/x type. So the options for heritability are 3/3, ¾, 3/x, and
> 4/x. I have a ¾ type and Benjamin has a 3/3 type. Allison and Christopher
> haven’t done their 23andme yet. GM and P are both 3/x. There is also a
> separate DQ type that is called DQ-8. Combining DQ-8 with DR3/4
> (heterozygous) gives you quite a high risk relative to the general
> population (estimates vary; with family history risk seems to be 10%-30%
> of CHILDHOOD diagnosis, lifetime risk is higher.) I have not seen the DQ-8
> gene in any family members’ genes I have looked at; we only have the
> hazardous DR genes. This tends to mean that diabetes shows up later than
> childhood, in early adulthood or later (but if you’re older than 40 your
> risk drops quite a bit, and it does taper off as you age.)
>
> You can use the flowchart below with your 23andme raw data to determine
> your OR. Remember, this is just a chance, not a diagnosis or prediction.
> There are environmental factors and other genes that influence risk
> INDEPENDENT of the established genetic risk, unfortunately, so your OR is
> probably higher than what is shown because we now have a “family history”.
> That effect is stronger with first degree relatives than cousins. You can
> also look at your predictions on 23andme for Celiac disease. If you have 2
> DQ-2.5 genes like Benjamin you are a 3/3. I It should also tell you if you
> have the Dq-8 variation. I have a ¾ with no DQ-8, so all it tells me is
> that I have a slight predisposition and one allele favoring development of
> celiac disease (the 3 they screen for.) If you go to your raw data you can
> search on the SNPs in the chart. You can also look them up on SNPedia,
> which is quite a rabbit hole. General pop risk is 1/300 so multiplying your
> OR by the general risk gives you an estimate. The percentages are
> prevalence in the general population. So far the results have been
> completely consistent with what I know about genetics, the independent
> 23andme predictions, and all the papers I have read.
>
> If you don’t want to fool with it just send me your 23andme login and I
> can do it for you.
>
> *The one thing most people on this list CAN do is sign up for a trial via
> TrialNet*. TrialNet will arrange a free blood test to detect antibodies
> in your blood that may be indicators of early diabetes. If they detect any
> concerning antibodies you can get free follow-up testing, and maybe avoid a
> stay in the ICU if you begin to get sick. They also use the data for
> research, trials and potential prevention of Type 1 so it is a highly
> worthy cause. Sign up at trialnet.org. You can do it if you have a
> relative as close as a cousin with Type 1.
>
> If you have questions I can likely answer them or send you research papers
> I have found helpful.
