When Coco Chanel said “elegance is refusal,” the fashion designer
recognized the power of a classic statement piece to command attention
without fussy accoutrements.

In watches, this philosophy applies to refined dress models that focus on
hours, minutes, and seconds—no fancy frills, no mind-boggling
complications, just pure expressions of time with a dose of modern dial
intrigue using color, texture, and even exotic materials.

Seeking to bring old-world elegance into the 21st century, H. Moser & Cie.
produces relatively small numbers of neo-vintage watches that blend the
contemporary with the classic. The family-owned brand—based in
Schaffhausen, Switzerland, and proud of its 100%-Swiss cred—quickly set
itself apart from the crowd with its unique fumé (smoked) colored dials,
with subtle shading that goes darker around the edges. The process to
produce just one of these dials is so involved that it requires more than
200 steps. Judging by the number of imitators in recent years, H. Moser
should be flattered.
Chopard L.U.C Flying T Twin Courtesy of Chopard

One new variation is this year’s Venturer Small Seconds XL in a luminous
shade called Arctic Blue (US$25,500), contrasted with a sizable 43mm
red-gold case, the largest made by the brand. Limited to 100 pieces, the
design is so pure and restrained that there are no numbers, no indices, and
not even the brand’s logo cluttering up the dial. In mastering the art of
understatement, H. Moser manages to be heard loud and clear.

While the vast majority of Moser dials are fumé, Moser has another range of
minimalist modern watches with dials made using Vantablack, a brand of
superblack coating that claims the world record for the darkest man-made
substance. Developed for use in aerospace, the material’s ability to absorb
ultraviolet rays endows the deep-black dials with an otherworldly quality.

Piaget, in creating a new version of its ultrathin Altiplano, looked to the
stars and came up with a range of dials made of genuine meteorite with
unique striations called Widmanstätten patterns, typical of the
crystallized nickel-iron structures found in iron asteroids.

Limited to 300 pieces in rose gold (US$24,600), the gray-meteorite version
punctuates the slice of space rock with slender rose-gold hour markers.
Piaget Altiplano with a meteorite dial Courtesy of Piaget

Stone dials have been a part of Piaget’s oeuvre for decades, as have
ultrathin movements—a challenging specialty that the brand pioneered with
its 1957 hand-wound caliber 9P that broke records for thinness. Piaget has
been investing in and advancing that legacy ever since. The latest
Altiplano is powered by the brand’s 1203P ultrathin automatic caliber,
which measures a mere 3mm thick, allowing it to fit comfortably in a
6.36mm-thick case that seamlessly slides under the sleeve of your bespoke
suit.

Chopard also slims down, with the sophisticated, limited-edition L.U.C
Flying T Twin (price upon request) in rose gold. Fifty pieces will be made
using ethically certified Fairmined rose gold, in keeping with the brand’s
commitment to advancing sustainable practices.

This handsome debut marks Chopard’s first automatic flying tourbillon
movement, the L.U.C 96.24-L, a COSC-certified chronometer that was
developed, produced, and assembled in the brand’s workshops. The ingenious
engine delivers 65 hours of power reserve with Chopard’s patented Twin
technology, which combines two coaxial barrels—a particularly challenging
feat, given that the movement is a just 3.3mm thick.

The watch’s perpetually spinning tourbillon floats in an aperture at 6
o’clock. And while the tourbillon is a mesmerizing, dynamic feature, its
only purpose is to maximize precision, so it’s technically not a
complication, even though it’s extremely complex to produce.

The solid gold dial is treated with a galvanic process to color it a shade
of ruthenium gray, a contemporary hue that complements the warm rose gold.
The dial’s center is engraved using a guilloché technique that imparts a
radiating beehive pattern in a nod to founder Louis-Ulysse Chopard’s
original logo.
